    When an Ohio parent passes and leaves behind a home, most heirs assume there's only one path forward: clean it out, fix it up, hire an agent, and put it on the market. For a lot of people, that path turns out to be longer, more expensive, and more stressful than anyone expected — and it isn't the only option.

    The part nobody adds up: what listing an inherited house really costs

    On paper, listing looks like the obvious move. In practice, an inherited home usually arrives with a stack of costs that come straight off the top before you ever see a check:

    • The cleanout. Decades of belongings, often with heirs living out of state, means dumpsters, movers, and weekends nobody has to spare.
    • The repairs. Older homes rarely pass a buyer's inspection as-is — roof, HVAC, plumbing, and cosmetics add up fast, and buyers ask for more after.
    • The holding costs. While it sits, the estate keeps paying property taxes, insurance, utilities, and upkeep — every single month.
    • The agent's cut. Commissions and closing fees come off the final price.
    • The uncertainty. Showings, then offers that collapse when a buyer's financing falls through, sending you back to the start.

    And in Ohio, there's often probate first

    An inherited house frequently has to move through probate before it can be sold. Depending on the estate, that can add weeks or months — and until it clears, the bills don't pause. For anyone who just wants the property off their plate, that limbo is often the hardest part.

    Why more owners are skipping the listing entirely

    That's why a growing number of Ohio homeowners are choosing a different route: selling the house exactly as it sits to a local cash buyer. No cleanout. No repairs. No commissions. They pick the closing date, and the sale can close in as little as a few weeks.

    It isn't the right call for everyone. A fully updated, empty, market-ready home may net more on the open market. But for a house that's dated, still full of belongings, or tangled in probate, the as-is route often leaves the owner with more in hand once repairs, commissions, and months of holding costs are subtracted from the "list it" column.
